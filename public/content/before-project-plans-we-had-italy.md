# Before Project Plans, We Had Italy

![Counter-Strike screenshot showing a cobbled junction, a narrow building between two rising paths, stone walls, and a player's rifle in the foreground.](/images/blog/counter-strike-italy.jpg "Photo source: Internet.")

*Before project plans, we knew these streets by heart.*

Look at this picture for a moment.

If you grew up playing Counter-Strike, you may already be choosing a route. You may also be remembering the friend you would want beside you.

For me, Italy brings back middle school, friends, and Counter-Strike 1.1. We kept playing into high school. Sometimes we even skipped classes for a game. That was part of our childhood too, however we feel about it now.

We wanted to play together. We did not know how long those afternoons would stay with us.

I remember the contest for space around the market. Someone had to reach a useful position quickly. Someone else had to cover him. If it worked, a small opening appeared and another friend could move forward.

There was a rhythm to it. Move too early and you could be on your own. Wait too long and the opening was gone. You had to pay attention to what your friends were doing, sometimes more closely than to what you wanted to do yourself.

Then came a sniper shot so good that the whole situation changed. Suddenly, the way ahead looked possible. Those were the moments we would remember. The friend quietly watching another approach mattered just as much, even if his part was less exciting to talk about.

Years later, I find myself recognizing that same dependence in renewable-energy delivery. Before one person can make visible progress, somebody else usually has to make it possible.

Imagine a transformer ready to leave the factory for a solar project. Procurement has arranged dispatch. The programme shows delivery, followed by installation. Everyone is trying to keep things moving.

Then the site supervisor says the receiving area is not ready.

There is a choice in how we respond. We can ask why site is behind and spend the meeting defending dates. Or we can find out what is missing, what has been checked, and what would make the delivery workable.

The second conversation gives us somewhere to go.

The supervisor knows the ground conditions. The logistics team knows what the delivery requires. The planner can see what a change would affect. Each holds part of the picture. Until they put those pieces together, everyone can be working hard toward a slightly different plan.

On Italy, each of us had a screen. A friend could see something that I could not. It made sense to listen to him, even when I thought I knew the map well.

That is an easy lesson to forget once we have job titles, responsibilities, and a plan we helped create. Yet the person closest to a problem may still be the first to see what needs to change.

Listening does not settle the decision. It gives the decision a better starting point.

For the transformer, perhaps a site walk reveals that the receiving area can be completed in time. Perhaps dispatch needs to move. Perhaps another approved, ready work area can progress while this one is resolved. We have to check the options, but first we have to allow ourselves to consider them.

I like that moment when a team stops repeating the problem and starts looking for a way through. The difficulty is still there. Now it has questions we can answer and actions we can take.

We learned to look beyond the obvious in the game too. I remember the deliberate shots through walls, trying to reach someone we believed was just out of sight. We were working from clues and our knowledge of the map. Sometimes the guess was right. Sometimes it was simply a guess.

Experience at work can be like that. A late equipment package may lead us to an unanswered design question. A testing delay may reveal that two teams each expected the other to prepare something. Recognizing the pattern helps us know where to look. We still need to check what is actually happening.

And when someone finds the answer, the work is not quite finished. The affected teams need to understand it and agree on what happens next.

A drawing can be complete while the people who must build from it still have questions. Equipment can be delivered while the crew that needs it is waiting for access. A plant can look finished while testing and handover remain unresolved.

These are the spaces between our tasks. They deserve as much attention as the tasks themselves.

Sometimes we need to move quickly to close them. Sometimes we need to slow down.

I remember the rounds when we fell back together. We had lost a useful position, and continuing to push was making things worse. Retreating gave us room to regroup, but it worked only if everyone understood. One friend still advancing while the others withdrew could undo the whole decision.

Changing pace at work takes the same care. If the receiving conditions are uncertain, holding the transformer at the factory may be sensible. But somebody must own the issue, agree when the decision will be made, and explain what allows dispatch to resume.

A pause becomes useful when the team knows what it is for.

There is a lesson here for life as well. We can remain committed to something while admitting that our current approach is failing. Slowing down may let us notice a choice we could not see while rushing.

Even choosing a pistol could mean adapting our play to the situation. We considered what we had and found a way to use it. In project work, a clear sketch and the right people in one conversation can sometimes resolve what another long report would leave open. The method has to fit the problem and meet the required standard. It does not always have to be elaborate.

The hardest games to forget are the ones when we were far behind and kept trying.

The gap could feel impossible. Then one better round gave us something to build on. We paid attention again. We changed what was going wrong. A friend who had struggled could still help the team in the next round.

That is the kind of persistence I want in a project team: people who can face a disappointing result and remain useful to one another.

A delayed project needs more than encouragement. It needs a credible next step, whether that is removing a constraint, changing the sequence, or being honest about a date that can no longer be met. But the way we treat each other affects whether those conversations happen early enough to help.

It is difficult to bring bad news to people who turn every setback into blame.

Some lessons from those games have stayed with me longer than answers I memorized for exams. School gave me knowledge I still use. Playing with friends gave me repeated experience of timing, trust, disappointment, and trying again. Their value became clearer when I began applying them thoughtfully to work.

The consequences are greater now. People's safety, time, livelihoods, and trust depend on our decisions. We owe each other more care than a game required.

Still, I am glad I remember Italy.

When I look at this picture, the market and the narrow streets come back quickly. So does the feeling of having friends beside me, each trying to make the next move work. We could lose a round and still have another chance together. At the time, that felt ordinary.

Today, the maps are drawings and programmes. There is still someone waiting for an opening, someone who can see a problem we have missed, and someone whose quiet work makes everyone else's progress possible.

Before I ask how fast we can move, I try to remember to ask who is ready, who needs help, and whether we are moving together.
